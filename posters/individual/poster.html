MY ARGUMENT · The pigs never have to win an argument. They decide what gets written down, quietly change it when it suits them, and let the other animals doubt their own memories. In Orwell’s farm, whoever holds the paintbrush holds the past.

THE STORY

The animals of Manor Farm drive out their drunken owner and paint Seven Commandments on the barn wall: all animals are equal. Slowly the pigs take control, and the rules quietly change. By the last page the other animals can no longer tell the pigs from the humans.

My review: Animal Farm ★★★★★ · SEPT 2026

I went into Animal Farm expecting a history lesson in costume: Napoleon is Stalin, Snowball is Trotsky, the windmill is the Five-Year Plan. That reading is accurate, and it’s the one most of us get taught. But by the last page, I felt it undersold the book. What unsettled me wasn’t the parallel to 1917. It was how easily the animals were talked out of their own memories.

Look at who can read on this farm. The pigs read perfectly. Muriel manages. Clover learns the alphabet but can’t put words together. Boxer, the strongest and most loyal animal, never gets past the letter D, and the sheep can’t hold even the Seven Commandments in their heads. So Snowball shrinks the whole philosophy into “Four legs good, two legs bad.” It looks like a kindness, a way to include everyone. It’s actually the first step toward a population that can only chant.

The line that stayed with me most is Squealer’s question when the animals half-remember a resolution against trade: “Is it written down anywhere?” It isn’t, so they decide they must have dreamed it. After that, the Commandments are quietly edited. Nobody lies about the past outright. The pigs just make sure the written record is the only past that counts, and then they hold the paintbrush.

READ IN 2026
Surrounded by edited posts and screenshots of claims that later vanish, the book feels less like a fable about Soviet Russia and more like a manual for anyone who controls the archive.
